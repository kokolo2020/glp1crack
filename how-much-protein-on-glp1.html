How Much Protein Should You Eat on GLP‑1?

Updated June 2026 · 6 min read

If you're on a GLP‑1 medication and eating far less than you used to, protein is the one nutrient you don't want to let slide. Here's a clear target and how to actually reach it.

The short answer

Most general nutrition guidance for adults who are losing weight while preserving muscle points to roughly 1.6 to 2.0 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. For someone weighing 80 kg, that's about 128–160 g of protein daily. If you prefer pounds, a rough rule of thumb is about 0.7–0.9 g per pound of body weight.

Why protein matters more on GLP‑1

GLP‑1 medications work largely by reducing appetite. That's the point — but a smaller appetite means it's easy to eat much less of everything, protein included. When you lose weight in a calorie deficit, some of that loss can come from lean muscle rather than fat. Eating enough protein, paired with some resistance exercise, is one of the most reliable ways to steer your body toward losing fat while holding on to muscle.

Muscle isn't just about looks. It supports your metabolism, strength, balance, and long-term ability to keep weight off. Protecting it during rapid weight loss is genuinely worth the effort.

This is general educational information, not medical or nutritional advice. Your needs depend on your health, kidney function, and other factors. Talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing your diet — see our full disclaimer.

The real challenge: hitting the number when you're not hungry

Knowing your target is easy. Eating it when a few bites fill you up is the hard part. A few practical strategies that people find helpful:

1. Eat protein first

When your appetite is small, the order you eat matters. Start each meal with the protein — the eggs, the chicken, the yoghurt — before bread, rice, or vegetables. That way the most important nutrient gets in before you feel full.

2. Spread it across the day

Your body uses protein more effectively in moderate amounts across several meals than in one big hit. Aiming for a portion at breakfast, lunch, and dinner — plus a snack — is easier on a small appetite than trying to cram it into one meal.

3. Lean on easy, dense sources

Some foods deliver a lot of protein in a small, easy-to-eat volume: Greek yoghurt, eggs, cottage cheese, fish, chicken, tofu, and protein shakes. A single scoop of protein powder in water or milk can quietly add 20–30 g when food just isn't appealing.

4. Drink some of it

On days when chewing feels like too much, liquids are your friend. A protein shake, a glass of milk, or a yoghurt smoothie can carry a big chunk of your daily target without much effort.

What protein looks like in real food

To make the number concrete, here's roughly how much protein common foods provide:

  • Large chicken breast — about 45 g
  • Salmon fillet — about 34 g
  • One egg — about 6 g
  • One cup Greek yoghurt — about 20 g
  • One scoop whey protein — about 25 g

So a 140 g daily target might be three eggs at breakfast (18 g), Greek yoghurt as a snack (20 g), a chicken breast at lunch (45 g), salmon at dinner (34 g), and a small shake to top up (25 g). It adds up faster than it looks.

A few sensible cautions

More protein isn't automatically better. Very high intakes aren't necessary for most people, and if you have kidney disease or another medical condition, your safe range may be different. This is exactly the kind of thing to confirm with your doctor or dietitian rather than guessing.

Bottom line

Aim for roughly 1.6–2.0 g of protein per kilogram of body weight, front-load it in your meals, and use easy dense sources when appetite is low. Pair it with a little strength training and you give yourself the best shot at losing fat while keeping the muscle that keeps the weight off.
